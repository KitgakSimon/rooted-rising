When Precious and I were little girls, we had a small habit that constantly got us into trouble. We used our school notebooks to draw. Not at the back pages where children are supposed to scribble, but right in the middle of the pages meant for notes. Our parents didn’t like it very much. To them, those books were for mathematics and English. But to us, they were canvases.

We were not drawing anything extraordinary. We were drawing the things we saw around us — people in the neighborhood, women in the market, fishermen by the creek, children playing on the streets. We didn’t know it then, but what we were trying to do was simple: we were trying to tell stories.

Stories of ordinary life.

As children, we also grew up reading storybooks. And those books had a strange power over us. They could make us believe almost anything. They could make us believe animals spoke, that heroes always won, and that the world made sense in the end. Stories shaped how we saw the world.

And that makes us think about something today.

Sometimes when people say they don’t believe the environmental realities around us — the polluted creeks, the dying mangroves, the health struggles in our communities — we often think about those childhood storybooks. Because storytelling has always been powerful enough to make people believe what they cannot see or refuse to see.

And maybe that is why art matters so much

if someone cannot see the truth in reports, data, or policy conversations, maybe they need to see it on a canvas.

Maybe they need to feel it in color.

Maybe they need to encounter it through art.

That is the spirit behind Painting the Change.

This campaign is about reclaiming our communities, owning our narratives, debunking false solutions, and challenging the systems of extractivism that have shaped our realities for decades.

For too long, other people have told the stories of our region. Too often those stories are incomplete, distorted, or disconnected from the lived experiences of the people who actually live here.

Through this project, we are saying something simple but powerful: We will tell our own stories.

And we will tell them through art.

Artists stood before blank canvases and slowly began to translate our realities into color, texture, and form. People walked around the room, watched the work unfold, asked questions, and reflected on what they were seeing.

In that moment, the museum was not just an art space.

It became a conversation about history, justice, environment, and hope.

And that is the power of art. It does not just decorate walls. It opens minds.

Today, as we continue this journey with the exhibition, we want to invite everyone here to see these paintings not just as artworks, but as stories. Stories about our communities, our environment, our resilience, and the future we want to shape together.

Beyond enjoying the art, we want to ask something simple from everyone here: support this vision. Support these artists. Support spaces where young creatives can tell the stories of our communities with honesty and courage.

At Rooted Rising, we believe movements grow when people come together to create, to question, and to imagine different futures.

And the artists in this room are helping us imagine those futures.

Dear Civil society actors, activists, academia, lawyers, community leaders, media, students and youths, as we stand among these stories on canvas, it is important that as we leave here, I hope we remember that movements begin when people dare to imagine something different.

The people of Ogoni once dared to imagine a world where communities could resist environmental destruction.

The people of Yasuní dared to imagine a world where oil could remain in the ground for the sake of life and the planet.

Those imaginations are not finished.

They are invitations.

As we stand among these stories on canvas, we invite all of us to carry that imagination forward. 

Ogonize the world!

Yasunize the world!

Thank you for coming and thank you to the Global Green Grant for making this possible.
